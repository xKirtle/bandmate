# Timeline placement rules are checked once, on what a change leaves

The Timeline's placement rules are checked once, on the Timeline a change leaves, never a Clip at a time partway through the change. The rules are: a Clip starts on the Timeline; no two Clips on a Track overlap; a Clip stays within its source; and its Fades fit it. Operations write their Clips without checking where they land. Every change runs through one path, which reads the Timeline before and after and hands both to one pure check, `checkLayout`. The check looks only at the Clips the change made or altered, so a Song whose stored layout is already slightly off never refuses the edit that would fix it. It returns which rule broke on which Clip, not a message, so a refusal could be put in an operation's own words; none needs to today.

**A Clip of Takes stays within its source for free**, so only a Clip of a Beat or a Sound can break that rule. Those play a file, which runs out: a Clip can't be trimmed out past the end of one, and never could. A Clip of Takes plays no file but the span its Takes are laid out in, and the span is an arrangement rather than a recording: it holds whatever the Clip plays, silence after the last Take included. Such a Clip may start before any Take has audio in it, and may end after the last one does.

That rule used to apply to a Clip of Takes as well, with the span ending where its last Take ends, and it made nudging lopsided. Nudging a Take later grows the span, which the Clip can then be trimmed out into; nudging it back asks for that room again, and the whole edit was refused, with "trim it first". So a Take could be nudged later as far as you liked and never brought back, though the same Clip would happily play twelve seconds of silence before its Take. The asymmetry came out of how the span's end was worked out, not out of anything musical: silence at a Clip's head was fine and silence at its tail was not. Letting the Clip keep its window costs the rule its grip on a Clip of Takes, so a trim out into silence is no longer refused by the server; the Timeline still stops the edge of such a Clip at where its Takes reach, or where the Clip already plays, whichever is further, so a nudge never yanks an edge the next drag touches. That is a UI bound now rather than a stored one, which is the price of the Take being as free to go earlier as it is to go later.

Checking each Clip as it was written made the order of writing matter. Moving several Clips had to store them all before checking any. Setting several Tempos had to go latest-first and pass along the Clips leaving. Getting that order wrong caused bugs, and the rules could only be tested by driving the whole HTTP server. Checking the result instead makes order irrelevant, and the rules are table-tested on plain Timeline values. The cost is reading the Timeline once more per change. ADR 0025 has since settled the write the same way: every change writes the whole Timeline through one writer, so write order is nothing an operation decides any more, and the hazards above are history. The database still refuses a Clip that starts before 0:00 or before its source, or that plays for no time, and those three were refused a Clip at a time as it was written, in the rule's own words; they are part of the one check now, which refuses them in the same words before anything is written. No operation should add per-Clip placement checks back. The searches for where a Clip fits (adding a Beat or a Sound, a Duplicate, a slowed Clip's new Track) find a place rather than refuse one. They search the Timeline the change starts from, testing for overlaps as the check does, never the database.
