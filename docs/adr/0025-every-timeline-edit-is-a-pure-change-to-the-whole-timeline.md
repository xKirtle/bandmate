# Every Timeline edit is a pure change to the whole Timeline

Every change to a Song's Timeline runs through one path: load the whole Timeline, make the change to it as a pure function of the Timeline, check the Timeline it leaves with one check, `checkLayout`, and write that through one writer, `writeTimeline`. It follows the Lyric Sheet's design (ADR 0024) and completes ADR 0023, which built the check half: the rules were already checked once, on what a change left, while every operation still wrote its own rows with its own SQL.

The writer compares the Timeline it's given with the rows stored, and inserts, updates and deletes only those that differ: Tracks (name, place from the top, volume, mute and solo), Clips (what they play, their name, their playback, and where they are and what of their source they play) and which Clip each Take is in and where it starts in that Clip's source span. It writes exactly the Timeline it's given, so **the order of writing is irrelevant**: an operation can build the Timeline it wants however it likes. A pure edit that adds a Track or a Clip takes its id from an id source it's handed, which counts up from each table's last-used id, and the writer inserts them as they are, so a Clip or Track brought back by Undo comes back as a new row. Those tables are AUTOINCREMENT, so an id is never used twice.

What a change brings with it from outside the Timeline is read, checked and written before the pure part, which is then handed the rows and only works out placement: a Take just recorded, with its audio and its number; an imported Sound; the copies a Duplicate or a paste makes of a Clip of Takes' Takes; and the Beat, Sound or detached Takes a Clip being placed is to play, which the Timeline doesn't hold until a Clip plays them. **No operation writes the Timeline's rows itself**, and no new one should. The one change outside the path is the Loop, which is the Song's own rather than a Track's or a Clip's. The three rules the database holds too — a Clip starts on the Timeline, and where its source does or later, and plays for some time — are part of the same check now, so nothing is checked a Clip at a time as it's written, and the database's own constraints are the backstop they always were.

The cost is one more whole-Timeline read per change, and a diff of it against the rows stored. What it buys is the same thing it bought the Lyric Sheet. Write order had to be documented and got wrong: setting several Tempos went latest-first and passed the leaving Clips along, and merging had to delete its Clips after they were read and add its new Tracks before placing the Clip. The same rules were enforced in two places, the searches for where a Clip fits existing once in SQL and once on the Timeline value, free to drift. And the behaviour of an operation was reachable only by booting the HTTP server and SQLite in a temporary directory, so each edit is now table-tested on plain Timeline values instead, the writer by writing a Timeline and loading it back, and the HTTP tests are the smoke tests that prove the path, the version check, file handling and refusal wording end to end.
